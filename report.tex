\section{Problem Setup}

The flood forecasting system implemented today relies on observing river gauges, which continuously measure the streamflow in cubic feet per second (cfs) \cite{usgs_nwis}. Each gauge has designated flood threshold assigned, when the streamflow exceeds this level, warning signals are issued to downstream communities \cite{wmo2011}. These systems are largely reactive, as they trigger on local conditions at individual gauges, but a flood wave entering a river takes several days to propagate from upstream to a downstream monitoring site \cite{lighthill1955, chow1988}. This lag in time provides an opportunity, upstream observations made today carry information about what a downstream gauge will record in the next few days. 

This report demonstrates a multi-day river flood forecasting as a linear least squares problem. The experiment uses historical streamflow and precipitation records across network of gauges along the Tar river basin, NC. The goal is to find a linear function of today's upstream observations that best predicts flow at a target gauge $k$ days from now. The matrix $A$ is built using observational features and a vector $b$ containing log-transformed flow at a downstream target gauge $k$ days ahead, for horizons $k \in \{1,2,3,4\}$, to solve: 

\[
  \min_{x \in \mathbb{R}^p} \|Ax - b\|_2^2.
\]

This least squares problem is solved using two methods; normal equations and QR factorization. The flood days are roughly $5\%$ of the data, so these are up-weighted by a factor of 10 using weighted least squares to prevent the solver from ignoring rare extreme events. Residuals on a held-out validation set generate empirical prediction intervals without distributional assumptions.

\section{Building the Design Matrix}

\subsection{Features and Normalization}
For this problem, real world precipitation, river gauge and network data are used at a daily time scale. The datasets were specifically chosen  such that they represent both local hydrological conditions at each gauge and upstream information \cite{usgs_nwis}. On each day $t$, the following measurements are observed and preprocessed into a feature
vector $a_t \in \mathbb{R}^p$:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
  \item Log-transformed flow at the target gauge: $\log(1 + Q_{\text{target}}(t))$
  \item Daily and rolling precipitation sums (7-day, 14-day) are antecedent moisture proxies gathered from total accumulated precipitation.
  \item Log-transformed flow at each upstream gauge: $\log(1 + Q_u(t))$ for $u \in \mathcal{U}$
  \item Sub-catchment precipitation between consecutive gauge pairs in the network order, daily and accumulated precipitation, spatially averaged over the sub-catchment polygon between those gauges.
\end{itemize}

Log transformation is applied to the streamflow values as the daily discharge is highly skewed and varies across several orders of magnitude. This reduces the influence of extreme flood peaks, preventing a small number of large events from dominating the squared-error loss.

All features are z-score normalized using training-set statistics. For each feature $j$:
\[
  \tilde{a}_{tj} = \frac{a_{tj} - \mu_j}{\sigma_j}
\]
where $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ are the training mean and standard deviation.
This ensures every feature lives on the same scale before forming $A^T A$.

\subsection{The Design Matrix}
The matrix $A$ consists of  $n$ normalized feature vectors stacked as rows, and a column of ones is added for the intercept. 

\[
A = \begin{bmatrix}
  1 & \tilde{a}_{1,1} & \cdots & \tilde{a}_{1,p-1} \\
  1 & \tilde{a}_{2,1} & \cdots & \tilde{a}_{2,p-1} \\
  \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
  1 & \tilde{a}_{n,1} & \cdots & \tilde{a}_{n,p-1}
\end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}
\]
The target vector collects log-flow $k$ days ahead:
\[
b = \bigl[\log(1 + Q_{\text{target}}(t_i + k))\bigr]_{i=1}^n \in \mathbb{R}^n.
\]

Four separate linear systems are constructed, one per horizon $k \in \{1,2,3,4\}$. The matrix $A$ is fixed across horizons; only the vector $b$ changes. The timeseries data for Tar river has $n = 7{,}178$ days, $p = 26$ input features (25 features $+1$ intercept).
The system is overdetermined by a factor of $\approx 276$, so no exact solution exists, suggesting the least-squares solution is the best attainable fit for this problem.

% ============================================================
\section{Least Squares}

\subsection{Derivation of the Normal Equations}
In this problem, as $n \gg p$, no $x$ satisfies $Ax = b$ exactly, so the fit is measured by total squared error. For each training day $i$,
the model produces a prediction $a_i^T x$ which is compared to the observed value $b_i$.
Summing squared per-day errors gives:
\[
  f(x) = \|Ax - b\|_2^2 = (Ax - b)^T(Ax - b).
\]
Squaring penalizes large errors disproportionately, missing a flood by 5,000 cfs is far worse than ten ordinary days each off by 500 cfs, and makes $f(x)$ smooth and differentiable. Expanding:
\[
  f(x) = x^T A^T A x - 2\, b^T A x + b^T b.
\]
This is a paraboloid bowl, positive semi-definite quadratic, single minimum, no local traps. By setting the gradient to zero:
\[
  \nabla_x f(x) = 2 A^T A x - 2 A^T b = 0,
\]
gives the \textbf{normal equations}:
\[
  \boxed{A^T A\, \hat{x} = A^T b.}
\]
This converts an unsolvable $7{,}178 \times 26$ system into a solvable $26 \times 26$ linear system.

\subsection{Three Solvers}

The least-squares problem can be solved several ways and they all return the same answer on well-conditioned data. But they might differ in how much numerical error they accumulate when the features are correlated, which happens to be the situation in this problem, as the upstream gauges along the same river tend to rise and fall together \cite{trefethen1997, golub2013}.

\paragraph{Normal equations.}
Form $A^TA$ explicitly and solve with Cholesky factorization:
\[
  \hat{x} = (A^TA)^{-1} A^T b.
\]
This method follows most directly from the gradient derivation, so it is a natural first approach. It works well when the columns of matrix \(A\) are independent, but it becomes less reliable when the features are highly correlated.

That issue appears in this dataset because nearby upstream gauges often respond to the same rainfall event. Their flow records rise and fall together, so the corresponding columns of \(A\) can become nearly parallel. Forming \(A^T A\) makes this problem worse: the small differences between similar features become harder to resolve numerically, and roundoff error can affect the final coefficients.

\paragraph{QR factorization.}
Factor $A = QR$ where $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ has orthonormal columns and
$R \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ is upper triangular. Substituting $A = QR$ into the
normal equations and using $Q^TQ = I$:
\[
  R^T Q^T Q R\, \hat{x} = R^T Q^T b \;\implies\; R\hat{x} = Q^T b.
\]
This is solved using back-substitution. Because $Q$ is orthonormal by construction,
$A^TA$ never needs to be formed explicitly, the problematic compression step is bypassed
entirely. QR is used as the primary solver throughout this project.

\paragraph{Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)}
Factor $A = U \Sigma V^T$ where $\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_p)$
contains the singular values. The least-squares solution becomes:
\[
  \hat{x} = V \Sigma^{-1} U^T b = \sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{u_i^T b}{\sigma_i} v_i.
\]

The SVD can be used to cross check as all the solvers should return the same $\hat{x}$, and verifying this to machine precision confirms the implementation is correct. Beyond verification, the singular value decomposition could also reveal which directions
in feature space carry the most variance in $b$, which could be used later for feature attribution via QR vector aggregation.

\section{Weighted Least Squares}
As mentioned earlier the flood days account for close to $5\%$ of total data, but the standard least squares weighs everyday equally. The unweighted solver effectively ignores this aspect of data, minimizing average error across 7,000 routine days.
This is fixed by assigning each day a weight $w_i$ via a diagonal matrix $W = \operatorname{diag}(w_i)$:
\[
  \min_x \|W^{1/2}(Ax - b)\|_2^2 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \min_x \|\tilde{A}x - \tilde{b}\|_2^2,
\]
where $\tilde{A} = W^{1/2}A$ and $\tilde{b} = W^{1/2}b$.
This is equivalent to the weighted normal equations:
\[
  (A^T W A)\,\hat{x} = A^T W b.
\]

The flood days (flow exceeding the 95th percentile, 8,641 cfs at the target gauge) are assigned a weight, $w_i = 10$ and $w_i = 1$ for all other days. In practice, when, $\tilde{A}$ and $\tilde{b}$ are formed explicitly and passed to the QR solver, flood-day rows appear with 10$\times$ more influence than ordinary rows, shifting the solution toward better coverage of rare extreme events.

This suggests that the upstream gauge readings provide useful information about future downstream flow that local data cannot capture alone. The residual grows with horizon for both models, which is expected as predicting four days ahead is simply a harder problem than predicting one day ahead.

After estimating $\hat{x}$ on the training set, the model is evaluated on a held-out validation set consisting of \emph{1461 daily observations}. For each validation day \(i\):
\[
  r_i = b_i - a_i^T \hat{x} \qquad (\text{log-space residual}).
\]
Positive residual suggests the model underpredicted; whereas negative means it overpredicted the flow values as shown in Table~\ref{tab:val_residuals}.
The empirical distribution of $\{r_i\}$ captures how wrong the model tends to be, without any distributional assumption.

\subsection{Prediction Intervals}

For a new observation $a_{\text{new}}$, the point forecast is $\hat{y} = a_{\text{new}}^T \hat{x}$.
The prediction interval at level $\alpha$ is:
\[
  \hat{y} + q_{\alpha/2} \;\leq\; y_{\text{new}} \;\leq\; \hat{y} + q_{1-\alpha/2},
\]
where $q_{\alpha/2}$ and $q_{1-\alpha/2}$ are the $(\alpha/2)$th and $(1-\alpha/2)$th
empirical quantiles of the validation residuals.
At $\alpha = 0.10$, this targets 90\% empirical coverage.

\begin{remark}
This is a marginal interval only: it uses the same quantiles regardless of input.
It works well on average but may be too narrow for out-of-distribution flood events
and too wide for low-flow days.
\end{remark}

\section{Results}

\subsection{Solver Agreement}

Both the solvers, normal equation and QR factorization produce the same weight vector $\hat{x}$ to within within $10^{-10}$ for both the local and upstream models at all the forecast horizons. This confirms the implementation is correct and that the system is well-conditioned enough
that numerical differences between solvers do not manifest at 64 bit precision.

\subsection{Forecasting Performance}

Table~\ref{tab:nse} reports the Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) and RMSE values for all the three models. The NSE measures the performance of the forecast, by comparing it with the observed streamflow relative to baseline, where the baseline is the performance forecast that assume future flow is same as the current flow. 

The upstream model outperforms both baselines at every horizon, and the margin seems to widen as the lead time grows here. At $t{+}1$, local flow alone is already a strong predictor because the river does not change much in a single day across the historical data. But by $t{+}4$,
local flow is essentially a stale reading, yet the upstream gauges still carry important information about the water wave that entered the network days earlier and has not yet arrived at the target site. That wave-routing signal indicates the fact that high
flow measured at an upstream gauge today will appear at the downstream gauge in
a few days. Adding lagged upstream gauge features helps the model anticipate downstream flow for the future, reducing four-day forecast error compared to using local information alone.

\section{QR Feature Attribution}

The QR factorization provides a clean way to attribute the target variance to individual
features without double-counting correlated predictors.
Factor $A = QR$ and define the coefficient vector:
\[
  c = Q^T b \in \mathbb{R}^p.
\]
Since $Q$ has orthonormal columns, $\|c\|^2 = \|Q^Tb\|^2$ equals the portion of $\|b\|^2$
explained by the column space of $A$. By Pythagoras, $c_i^2$ is the contribution of the
$i$-th orthogonalized direction to the total explained variance:
\[
  s_i = \frac{c_i^2}{\sum_{j=1}^p c_j^2} \in [0,1], \qquad \sum_{i=1}^p s_i = 1.
\]
This is a true variance decomposition as each $s_i$ is the marginal share attributable to
direction $i$ after the previous directions have been accounted for.

The first entry ($i = 1$) corresponds to the bias column and is discarded for interpretation. The remaining $s_i$ are renormalized to sum to 1 and reported as the feature importance bar chart. The attribution ordering depends on the column ordering in $A$ as QR is not permutation-invariant, but the aggregate shares by feature group (local flow, upstream flow, precipitation) are stable.

The flow value at the target gauge is the single strongest predictor of near-future flow under normal conditions as demonstrated in Table~\ref{tab:qr_contrib}, which is as expected. The reported observation is its share falls steadily from 95.9\% at $t{+}1$ to 87.0\% at $t{+}4$, and that slack is shifted to the first-in-the-network upstream gauge 02081500. Its growing contribution at longer leads directly reflects the physics of flood wave propagation, a surge recorded there today arrives at the target site in approximately four days.

\section{Conclusion}

This project framed multi-day flood forecasting as a linear least-squares problem and used it as a way to connect the linear system modeling methods to real world streamflow sensor data. The mathematical solvers produce similar fitted coefficients, but differ in numerical behavior. 

The results demonstrate that the choice of objective matters as well, when flood days are weighted more heavily, the fitted model shifts toward better performance during high-flow periods, but this could come at some cost to normal flow day behavior. The residual based prediction intervals provide a simple way to quantify uncertainty without assuming a specific error distribution.

The model captures useful relationships between lagged upstream gauges and downstream flow, but would fail to represent non-linear flood processes such as backwater effects, dam operations or the threshold behavior during rare tail end events. Future work could use non linear or more sequence based models while maintaining the structure of input and output features.
